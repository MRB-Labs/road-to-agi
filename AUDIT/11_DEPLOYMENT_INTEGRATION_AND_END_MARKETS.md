# Commercial Section — Deployment, Integration and End Markets

## Classification

This content is strategically essential but **must not be presented as Layer 11 of the physical architecture**. It is the commercialisation, adoption and value-realisation system surrounding Layers 1–10.

The section answers:

- Who buys the system?
- Which business problem is being solved?
- Who integrates and operates it?
- Who carries implementation and liability risk?
- How is value measured and monetised?
- Who captures the economic surplus?

## 1. Current chain

No complete chain existed in the original website. Operators, tenants, buyers and deployment appeared inconsistently as final stages inside other technical layers.

A provisional chain is:

Customer problem → Feasibility → Pilot → Integration → Approval → Deployment → Operations → Measured outcome → Renewal/expansion

## 2. Main problems and missing components

### Technical production and adoption are mixed

A product can be technically available but commercially undeployable because of integration cost, regulation, liability, workflow disruption, procurement or insufficient ROI.

### The buyer is not always the beneficiary

Distinguish:

- Economic buyer
- Technical buyer
- Operator
- End user
- Person affected by the system
- Regulator or approval authority
- Insurer or liability bearer

### Missing adoption stages

- Problem definition and baseline measurement
- Technical and economic feasibility
- Vendor selection and procurement
- Site/workflow readiness
- Data and systems integration
- Pilot and acceptance criteria
- Security, privacy, legal and regulatory review
- Safety case and certification where relevant
- Organisational design and change management
- User training and operating procedures
- Production deployment
- Monitoring, support and incident response
- Outcome measurement
- Renewal, expansion, replacement or termination

### Missing value-chain participants

- Consultants and engineering firms
- Systems integrators
- Managed-service providers
- Distributors and channel partners
- Industry-specific software providers
- Robot integrators and automation specialists
- Training and change-management providers
- Certification, testing and audit organisations
- Insurers and financiers
- Maintenance, repair and lifecycle-service providers

### Statements to avoid

- “If the technology works, adoption follows.”
- “The largest technical moat captures the most value.”
- “Labour cost equals addressable revenue.”
- “A successful pilot proves scalable economics.”
- “Automation eliminates an entire job.”
- “The buyer and end user have the same incentives.”

## 3. Recommended structure and corrections

## Commercial models

For each use case, compare:

- Hardware sale
- Software subscription
- Usage-based API or compute pricing
- Per-seat pricing
- Per-device or per-robot licence
- Robotics-as-a-service
- Managed service
- Transaction- or outcome-based pricing
- Systems-integration project revenue
- Maintenance and lifecycle contracts
- Leasing, financing and capacity reservation

## Unit-economics framework

Measure:

- Customer acquisition and deployment cost
- Hardware and infrastructure cost
- Integration and training cost
- Gross margin by revenue stream
- Human supervision and exception-handling cost
- Utilisation and uptime
- Payback period
- Customer switching cost
- Renewal and expansion rate
- Liability, warranty and support burden
- Working-capital and financing requirements

For embodied systems, include cost per productive hour, intervention rate, maintenance, energy, consumables, insurance and residual value.

## Value creation versus value capture

For every market, distinguish:

- Technical value created for the customer
- Portion retained by the customer
- Portion captured by the model, software, hardware, infrastructure and integration providers
- Competitive forces likely to compress each margin pool

A technically essential supplier may capture little value if its product is substitutable. An integrator or application provider may capture more if it owns the customer workflow and outcome.

## Connection to the ten-layer architecture

This section should sit beside the physical map, not above it. It receives products and capabilities from Layers 1–10 and connects them to paying demand.

- Layers 1–6 supply infrastructure capacity.
- Layers 7–9 supply data, intelligence and digital workflows.
- Layer 10 supplies physical automation.
- Deployment and integration determine whether those capabilities produce measurable outcomes.
- End-market economics determine willingness to pay and sustainable returns.

## Required verification

Every market-size or productivity claim should specify the use case, customer, geography, adoption rate, price, implementation cost, time horizon and source. Avoid calculating opportunity solely by multiplying jobs or wages by an assumed automation percentage.
